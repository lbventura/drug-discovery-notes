1. Target discovery

The first step in drug development is target discovery - find the targets for treating or preventing a disease. These **targets are usually proteins in the patient's body,** either associated with the disease or with micro-organisms related to the disease.

Understand protein pathways is relevant to target discovery.

2. Drug discovery

**Find chemical compounds that bind the identified target**. If the compound modulates the target in a way that alters the disease behavior, this matching compound (called hit) will be modified such that it is both safe and effective.

Of 10k hits, only 1 may lead to a drug that reaches the market.

3. Safety and Drug Metabolism

Late pre-clinical stage. Checks appropriate absorption and metabolism.

4. Clinical phase I-II

I - tested in healthy volunteers to understand safety and pharmacokinetics
II - tested in 100-250 patients with the disease to understand safety, efficacy and the optimal dose

5. Clinical phase III

About 1000-3000 patients are recruited to test the investigational drug.

## Oral Semaglutide in Adults with Overweight and Obesity

**Q1: What is semaglutide?** Anti-diabetic medication used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes and an anti-obesity medication used for long-term weight management. It is a **peptide** similar to the **hormone glucagon-like peptide-1** (GLP-1), modified with a side chain.

**Q2: What are the proven benefits of semaglutide for patients with obesity?**
- Reduction of fat mass without the degradation of lean muscle mass.
- Reduction of cardiovascular risk.
- Reversal of liver fibrosis.
- Reduction of inflammation, as semaglutide normalizes metabolic telemetry.

**Q3: What is the dose, the dosing frequency and the route of administration that has been shown to show a benefit?**
Traditionally this is done via subcutaneous route to avoid the protein degradation in the stomach's acidic environment. In this case, the standard dose is 2.4 mg weekly, with a high dose capped at 7.2 mg. This is administered in step-up fashion.

However, oral route is now possible when semaglutide is co-formulated with the absorption enhancer SNAC. Because it has low absorption efficiency, the once-daily pill contains a dose of either 25 or 50 mg.

**Q4: What is the goal of the current clinical study?**
The primary goal was to evaluate the efficacy, safety and tolerability of a 25 mg once-daily oral dose of semagluitide compared to a placebo in adults with overweight or obesity. **The current study aimed to prove that a lower-dose** (25mg compared to the 50mg) **can still drive adiposity reduction** and **cardiometabolic benefits.**

Q5: How many patients participated in the trial?
The trial enrolled a total of 307 participants, using a 2:1 randomization schema:

205 randomly assigned to the active experiement
102 to the control

The cohort was composed by 79% women and 92% white, with a mean baseline of 48y.

**Q6: What were the coprimary outcomes of the trial?** (Explain what are coprimary outcomes)

Coprimary outcomes - In clinical biostatistics, **coprimary outcomes** (or endpoints) refer to a **study design where two or more distinct primary objects are evaluated simultaneously.** For the trail to be designated a sucess by regulatory agencies, the study must demostrate statistically significant results across both of the predefined endpoints.

In obesity drug development, these specific coprimary endpoints are standard FDA requirements:

1. mean % weight loss, which proves the general magnitude of the drug's effect across the entire distribution of patients,
2. binary >5% responder threshold, which proves clinical relevant. A 5% reduction in body mass is the established threshold at which systemic cardiometabolic paramters (blood pressure, lipids profile, glycemic control) begin to improve.


**Q7: What was the most common adverse event? How many patients experienced that?**

Because the **GLP-1 receptor** agonists **delay gastric emptying** and **act on the hindbrain's satiety and nausea centers**, the most common treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were gastrointestinal (GI) events, in particular nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and constipation.

- In the oral semaglutide (25 mg) group, 74% of participants experienced these gastrointestinal side effects,
- In the placebo group, 42% of participants reported similar baseline complaints.

Despite the high incidence rate, the study notes that these events were primarily mild-to-moderate in severity, transient, and occurred predominantly during the 12-w initial dose-escalation phase.

**Q8: What was the conclusion of the authors?**

Oral semaglutide at 25mg once-daily provides a highly effective alternative to both injectable semaglutide and 50mg.

- The active drug drove an estimate mean body weight change of ~13.6% at week 64,
- The active group outperformed the placebo in large body weight changes: 30% of the patients in the active arm achieved >20% weight loss compared to just 3%

Q10: What questions do you have?

- What changes were introduced to the pill such that it displays large effectiveness with a smaller dose (25 mg vs 50 mg)? A: No changes were introduced. Both formulations use the same absorption-enhacing excipient: 300 mg of SNAC. Clinical data revealed that **25 mg provides sufficient systemic plasma exposure** to **saturate the GLP-1 receptors in the hindbrain** required for appetite suppression. **The 50mg dose yields slightly higher weight loss, but the 25 mg dose captures the vast majority of that benefit.**
- In what country was the study conducted? A: It was conducted in 4 countries; the US, Canada, Germany and Poland.
- Since the cohort is mostly composed of white middle-aged women, are the results general enough? Have these been verified by other studies? A: These results have been cross-verified by large clinical programs on semaglutide. The efficacy of the GLP-1 RA mechanism does not exhibit significant racial, ethnic or sex-based dimorphism. The OASIS 4 trial serves primarily to validate the pharmacokinetics of the 25 mg oral delivery route, relying on the broader semaglutide data registry to prove systemic generalizability.
- What therapy can be introduced, together with the semaglutide, to minimize the gastrointestinal side-effects? A: Because TEAEs are heavily dependent on the drug C_{max} (peak plasma concentration) and its mechanism of delaying gastric emptying, **mitigation** relies on a mix of **titration** (12-w dose escalation, starting at a 3 mg microdose, allowing the central nervous system and gastric pathways to build tolerance), **dietary intervention** (consume smaller, more frequent meals, restric high fat, high fiber foods) and **adjunctive pharmacotherapy** (for nausea, ondansetron and for constipation, laxatives). 
- What is the drug-delivery efficiency of the 25 mg pill compared to the injectable version? A: Bioavalability for the injectable dose is 89%. For the oral pill, about 0.8-1%.

## Orfoglipron

**What is Orfoglipron?** A: a novel, oral, non-peptide, **small molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist**. Instead of mimicking the biological peptide structure of the human GLP-1 hormone, orfoglipron is a chemically synthesized small molecule designed to **bind to and activate the exact same GLP-1 receptors** in the pancreas, gut and hindbrain.

**What are its differences and similaries to semaglutide?** A: 
The similarities are in the pharmacodynamics: both molecules are strong agonists of the GLP-1 receptor. Because this mechanism of action is identical, the adverse events are also nearly identical. The differences appear in molecular engineering and pharmacokinetics: semaglutide is a peptide (a large chain of amino acids) whereas orfoglipron is a small molecule. Whereas oral semaglutide is severely limited by biological degradation, it requires a heavy chemical buffer (SNAC) to temporarily alter stomach pH and force ~1% of the peptide into the bloodstream. Orfoglipron, being a small molecule, is more bioavailable in the gastrointestinal tract and does not require an absorption-enhancing excipient.

Synthesizing peptides requires complex recombinant DNA technologies, specialized bioreactors and extensive purification processes. Small molecules like orfoglipron can be manufactured via standard synthetic chemistry.

## Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1)

GLP-1 is a 30- or 31-amino acide long peptide. It is derived from a protein encoded by the GCG human gene. 

- It helps **regulate appetite** and **lower glucose**,
- Acts at the **hypothalamus** to **increase satiety**,
- Acts on the **gut** to slow **GI motility** and **increase satiety**,
- Acts on the **pancreas** to **upregulate insulin secretion** from pancreatic beta cells

Endogenous GLP-1 has a half-life of about 2 mins, making its natural form a poor drug candidate due to its pharmacokinetic behavior.

Synthesized agonists have similar sequences but much longer half-life due to chemical modifications. 

Examples include:

- **Semaglutide** (approved). Differs from semaglutide in 2 amino acids such that it can reversibly bind to serum albumin in the body,
- **Taspoglutide** (tested, not approved). Idem

### What is an amino acid?

Amino acids are organic compounds that serve as the building blocks of proteins. The body uses them to grow, repair tissues, synthesize hormones, and produce energy. 

There are **20 different amino acids** that combine in various sequences to create thousands of unique proteins. Broadly categorized into two groups:
1. **Essential Amino Acids** - Your body cannot make these on its own, so you must get them directly through your diet. The 9 essential amino acids are Histidine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Threonine, Tryptophan, and Valine.
2. **Non-Essential Amino Acids** - Your body can naturally synthesize or produce these amino acids, so they are not strictly required from food. Examples: Alanine, Asparagine, Aspartic Acid, Glutamic Acid, and Serin.

## GLP1R, a receptor of GLP-1

GLP1R is a GPCR receptor found on beta cells of the pancreas, on neurons of the brain, as well as in the gastrointestinal tract. It is involved in the regulation of blood sugar level by enhancing insulin secretion (incretin signaling).

Native GLP-1 is an incretin hormon secreted by the L-cells of the intestines immediately after eating, signalling the body to prepare for an influx of glucose. Because semaglutide saturates the (same) GLP-1 receptors in the pancreas and gastrointestinal tract, semaglutide rewires the broken negative feedback system loop:

1. Semaglutide binds to the degraded beta cells in the pancreas and activates a secondary messenger pathway, which restores the cells the cell's ability to produce insulin. Importantly, this gain is glucose-dependent. The cell only releases insulin if it simultaneously detects that blood glucose is above the physiological setup.
2. Semaglutide blocks the erroneous positive feedback loop by suppressing the alpha-cells, stopping the liver from inappropriately dumping glucose into the bloodstream.
3. By slowing down the motility of the stomach, semaglutide flattens the rate at which glucose enters the bloodstream after a meal. Instead of glucose spike that impacts the pancreas, the system experiences a more manageable profile.

## Semaglutide v Liraglutide

**Liraglutide** has 97% sequence homology to native GLP-1.

- Replaces arginine for lysine P 34,
- Attaches a C16 fatty acid (palmitic acid) to P 26 via a simple glutamic acid spacer
- The C16 chain allows it to reversibly bind to human serum albumin (HSA) in the blood, slowing renal clearance and extending its half-life from 2 min to 13h. 

**Semaglutide** has 94% sequence homology to native GLP-1, introducing t**wo upgrades**:
- Enzymatic immunity (Aib8): at P 8, semaglutide substitutes alanine with alpha-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib). **Aib creates steric hindrance**, thereby **physically blocking the DPP-5 enzyme from docking** and cleaving the peptide's N-terminus.
- C18 Diacid & PEG Spacer: Semaglutide replaces the C16 chain with a longer C18 fatty diacid, attached via a complex hydrophilic PEG spacer. This creates a strong non-covalent bond with albumin.

As a result, the half-life is extended to 165 hours.

**Why does higher half-life drives superior weight loss?**

**1. Pharmacokinetic stability**: Because liraglutide has a 13h half-life, receptor saturation drops considerably before the next injection, such that the patient experiences hunger. The semaglutide 165h half-life leads to a nearly constant plasma concentration after 4-5 weeks. The hindbrain receptors are saturated 24/7, providing constant appetite suppression.
**2. Enhanced Blood-Brain Barrier penetration**: Weight loss is governed primarily by the brain. **The C18 diacid and PEG spacer alter semaglutide's lipophilic/hydrophilic balance**. This change in spacial conformation allows semaglutide to **cross the blood-brain barrier much more efficiently than liraglutide.** Therefore, it **achieves a higher local concentration in the deep satiety centers of the hypothalamus.**

Semaglutide is superior not because it is a "stronger" agonist at the receptor level, but because the Aib8 and C18 modifications create a better pharmacokinetic profile: immunity to enzymatic cleavage, a flat steady-state concentration curve, and the ability to utilize active transport mechanisms to physically reach deeper satiety networks in the hypothalamus than liraglutide can.
